Most developers I know are better thinkers than they are communicators. They can hold complex systems in their head, debug gnarly race conditions, design clean APIs — and then struggle to explain what they're doing or why to anyone who wasn't in the room.

Writing publicly is one of the most effective ways to close that gap. And I think most developers underrate how much it compounds.

## The obvious benefit

Writing forces clarity. You can't hide vague understanding behind fast speech or nodding in meetings. The sentence either makes sense or it doesn't.

I've sat down to write posts where I thought I understood something well and realized midway through that I had a gap I hadn't noticed. The writing found the gap. That's the tool working.

## The less obvious benefit: it accumulates

A post you write today might be the most useful thing someone reads in two years. You probably won't know who — that's the point.

Public writing creates a trail of your thinking over time. The developers I've learned the most from aren't the ones with the biggest audiences — they're the ones who wrote clearly and consistently about things they actually cared about. Six posts a year on things they genuinely figured out. That kind of writing has a long shelf life.

Shallow content optimized for traffic decays quickly. Honest writing about real problems — written for the version of you who needed it six months ago — doesn't.

## The real reason most of us don't do it

It's not time. Most developers who say "I don't have time to write" would find time if writing felt as urgent as shipping code.

The real reason is stakes. Writing publicly feels vulnerable in a way that writing code doesn't. Code either works or it doesn't — the test suite is the arbiter. Writing invites judgment on your thinking, your clarity, your intelligence. That's uncomfortable.

The way I've reframed it: I'm not writing for an audience, I'm writing for my past self. I'm documenting what I figured out so that when I — or someone in exactly my situation six months ago — goes looking, they find it. That framing removes the performance anxiety. The post doesn't need to be impressive. It just needs to be useful.

## Starting is the hard part

Most developers who start writing quit after two or three posts because the early posts get no engagement. That's normal and irrelevant.

The value of writing isn't the immediate feedback loop — it's the compounding effect of a body of work over time. Post ten: the tenth is noticeably better than the first. Post fifty: you've built a searchable record of two years of learning that you own and that exists independently of any platform.

The writers who compound are the ones who keep going when early posts are quiet. The bar isn't "did this go viral" — it's "did I write something that was true and useful."

Write the post. Nobody has to read it today.
